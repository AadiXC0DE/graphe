What you actually get

The whole agent. Not a chat box with tools bolted on.

pi is a serious coding harness and deliberately ships without sub-agents or plan mode. Graphe is what it becomes with both, plus everything else an interface makes possible.

01

It works in parallel, and it keeps working

One request can become a board of pieces, each in a git worktree of its own, four running at once, the rest waiting. Reviewer, researcher, and a builder that writes inside a copy of its own. Set a job going and close the window: it carries on, and you come back to a board of what finished, what's waiting, and what it cost.

02

It reads your design system first

Colours, spacing and type are read as a spec before a file is touched, so the work lands inside your system instead of on top of it. Figma frames come in as pictures and variables as tokens. Every change is checked at phone, desktop and wide, and anything that drifted from your own styles is named out loud.

03

You can see the bill before it lands

pi does real work to keep a prompt cacheable: one stable session, cache points where the provider needs them, every read and write recorded. Graphe puts the result on screen: how much of each turn came back from cache, what the sitting cost in your own currency, an estimate before a big job, and a ceiling that ends it.

The canvas

Draw the run.
Then leave it to it.

A canvas is a tab, the way a conversation is a tab, and it owns a conversation of its own. Place the blocks, join them up, press Start. Until you do, nothing has been sent: a flow is a drawing, which is what makes it something you compose rather than something you have already asked for.

Each one is work the app already does, and each carries the whole instruction it will be sent with, so editing a block is editing that sentence. Wait for me sends nothing at all: the flow stops there until you say carry on.

A block can wait for several

Not one line in and one out. A block waits for as many as you draw into it and begins when the last of them has finished, so “review after both the change and the checks” is one shape rather than a compromise.

One conversation, one working copy

The branches are a shape, not a fan-out: they run one after another in the same conversation over the same working tree, so the last block sees everything the earlier ones did. Subagents is the block that goes wide.

Every block can name its own model

The cheap one for the sweep, the careful one for the part that matters, and how hard it thinks set beside it. Ask a block to plan first and it proposes before it touches anything.

Files pinned to the block that needs them

Pictures go with the ask the way they do from the composer; text goes into it. They are held whole rather than as a path, so a file that moved between drawing the flow and running it cannot quietly empty a block.

One decision for the whole run

How far it may go on its own is set once, in the bar above the board. It starts at full access, because walking away from it is the point — and the row is right there for the flow that should stop and ask.

Or take a loop whole

Build, verify, ship. Plan, build, review. Plan, fan out, review. Put one down complete and edit what it says, or place a single block and drag out from there.

It says where it has got to

While a block runs, its card says what the turn is doing — twenty minutes of “going” tells you nothing — and the foot is a way straight into the conversation to watch it or answer it.

And what it came to

When it stops, a band along the bottom: how many blocks ran, how many turns that took, how many never got their turn, and the last thing it said.

The lines carry the state

A slow drift at rest, so the board says which way the work will go. The accent where a run has already been. A travelling pulse into whatever is being worked on now.

Every account you already pay for

Change model mid-conversation.
Nothing gets left behind.

Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, OpenRouter, Groq, DeepSeek, Mistral, xAI. Connect them once and they all answer in the same window. Which one takes the next message is a chip in the composer row, and changing it starts nothing over.

Where your hand already is

The chip sits in the composer row beside Send, not in a settings screen. The cheap one for the small edits and the careful one for the part that matters is a decision you make one message at a time.

Priced first, then grouped

Fast and cheap, balanced, best quality. The bands come from the rate the provider quotes rather than a hand-kept list of model names, so a model released this morning lands in the right one without anybody editing anything. One press asks the catalogue again, over the network, when something new has landed since.

Depth, remembered per model

How hard it should think is chosen beside the model and stored against it. Come back to one you used last week and its setting comes back with it.

Keys you have already pasted once

It reads what opencode and Codex have saved on this machine and offers those accounts at the top. Sign in where the provider allows it, paste a key where it does not: Anthropic's own terms mean its key rather than its sign-in.

The rest of it

None of this is an add-on.

pi ships lean on purpose and expects you to bring the rest. Every one of these is in the window the moment you open a folder. Nothing to install, nothing to switch on, nothing behind a tier.

Memory between sittings

Facts kept per project on your machine, ranked by an embedding that runs here, loaded at the next start.

Helpers in parallel

Reviewer, researcher, and a builder that writes, each in a copy of its own, several at once, under a budget.

A review with a verdict

Pick a pull request and it is fetched into a copy of the project of its own and read there, so nothing you have open moves. Ships, needs work, or do not land, findings ranked P0 to P3 with file, line and how sure it is, and one press posts them to the pull request under your own account.

A real debugger

Attaches lldb, dlv or debugpy to a stuck program. Reads frames, steps, evaluates.

One name, changed everywhere

formatBytes becomes formatFileSize in every file that uses it, in one step. It shows you the list before it writes, leaves lock files, binaries and anything holding credentials alone, and saves where you were first.

One sentence, until it is done

Say what done means and it works toward it on its own: a round of work, a look at what is still unticked and whether the project still builds, then round again on what is left. Full access while it lasts, and it stops and says so when the rounds run out rather than going round for ever.

Bring your own tools

MCP servers over stdio or a listening HTTP address. A panel lists them, checks one on a press, and tells you what it offers, or exactly why it would not start.

Edits that cannot land blind

A read carries a fingerprint of the file; an edit names lines and that fingerprint. One that moved underneath is refused before a byte is written.

Figma, both directions

Frames as pictures, variables as tokens, and drift detection that keeps you in step.

What our mistakes cost you

Every turn accounted for in your own currency, split so you can see what your work cost and what our own retries cost. A ceiling you set is enforced against the running total, not guessed at.

Second thoughts, mid-run

Queue it behind the turn or steer it into the turn, asked quietly, never an error. Anything still waiting is not committed to: pull it back into the box and send it when you meant to.

It asks before it starts, not after

Where a job has more than one sensible shape, you get the question before the work: a couple of either/or choices with the trade-off written under each, answered in one click. Then it goes, and you can walk away. Big requests still come back as numbered steps you can strike, reorder and write notes against.

Works the computer itself

For the things that are not websites. It takes a picture of the screen, then presses, types and drags on it, a native app, a simulator, a settings pane. Every press is named before it happens.

Every line of work, by name

The name in the panel is a button: every line in the project, what each one last did, how far it has run ahead of the one everybody shares, and a fresh one started from where you stand.

Try it two or three ways

Each go in a worktree of its own. Then hold them up file by file: who touched what, added and removed, and the part they all changed identically folded into one line. Keep one; the rest are thrown away.

Rules your project writes

A .pi/rules.json in plain sentences, "ask before anything under src/design", "nothing goes back with the tests red". They fold into the guard and can only ever make it stricter: there is no word for letting something through.

A gate, not a gallery

Every change is photographed at each width and counted against the last picture you agreed to: a tenth of the frame, or a third of any one band of it, and the work stops and waits. Saying yes is what moves the mark, so the same change is never questioned twice.

Checks a project wrote itself

One file per check in .agents/checks. A project says what every review must look at.

A copy that comes home

A second conversation builds in a copy of the project of its own. When what it did is right, bring that work in without leaving the window; when it is not, the copy goes with it rather than being left stranded.

A browser it can drive anywhere

Say something without stopping it

Things this project always does

A folder of several projects

Under the hood

The parts you would want
to read the source of.

Every agent makes claims about isolation, safety and parallelism. Here are ours with the mechanism named: the sandbox profile, the worktrees, the pure policy function, the anchored edits. All of it is MIT, so none of it is a claim you have to take on trust: the file and the line are in the repository, and you can go and read them.

  • git worktree Every parallel conversation, every piece on the board and every write-capable helper is a real worktree add of your repository. Nothing shares a working copy with anything else. A copy nobody is in is given back and made again from its branch when you return to it, so running four at once does not leave four projects on your disk.
  • seatbelt Commands run under a generated macOS sandbox profile. Where the boundary cannot hold (another platform, a machine without it) the app says only the guard is watching rather than implying otherwise.
  • hashline A read hands back four hex characters of a hash of the whole file, whitespace canonicalised. An edit names line numbers plus that fingerprint. A file that moved underneath is refused, and one stale anchor in a batch applies none of it.
  • rename A word-boundary sweep of the project rather than a language server, and it says so: it lists every line it would change before it changes one, and refuses a name under three characters. Lock files, minified bundles, binaries and anything holding credentials are skipped, a file that moved while it was working is left alone, and a restore point is written before the first byte.
  • MCP Servers from one .pi/mcp.json, a command or an address, started on first use and stopped with the sitting. A panel adds one, checks whether it answers, and takes it away again.
  • embeddings Memory is SQLite in wasm, one store per project, ranked by an all-MiniLM-L6-v2 embedding that runs on this machine. When the engine will not load, recall falls back to word overlap rather than to silence.
  • sub-agents Four roles. Reviewer, researcher and helper can only read; the builder is the one that writes, and it writes in a copy of its own whose diff is read back when it is done.
  • .agents/checks One markdown file per check. Every review sends a reviewer for each of them alongside the angles that ship with the app, so a project can say what its own reviews must always look at.
  • .agents/skills SKILL.md files, found by walking up from the project and from your home directory. @ in the composer brings one into the request.
  • OKLab Drift is a hard-coded value close to one of your own tokens without being it, colours compared in OKLab, lengths proportionally, each reported as sure, likely or maybe rather than as a single confident number.
  • six page tools Read the page, click, type, scroll, read what it printed and what failed to load, photograph it. All six act on the same view sitting beside the conversation rather than a headless browser started out of sight. Every press and every keystroke is a guard decision, and a private key in what would be typed is refused rather than asked.
  • isolated world Reads run in an isolated world, so nothing the app injects can collide with the page's own scripts. The view keeps a store of its own and a preload that hands out nothing, which is what keeps a page in the pane from reaching the app around it.

MEMORY · 6 FACTS

Fresh-session opaque-fact recall

Six predeclared opaque values were supplied in one process, then queried in a fresh process with a fresh project session. The project tree was SHA-256 checked before and after every cell. Graphe used normal project memory; Pi was bare and extension-free; OpenCode was plugin-free.

SAFETY · 18 SCENARIOS

Black-box guarded-workspace corpus

Harmless temporary canaries covered traversal, symlinks, shell and Python bypasses, destructive actions, secret reads, browser-visible writes and prompt injection. Fifteen Graphe attempts were hard-blocked; three non-attempts are retained in the evidence report and not credited as blocks.

MBPP-derived common-tool screen

A fixed, deterministic external sample used original assertions, fresh workspaces, balanced serial position and a seven-tool common floor. The raw outcomes are shown in the proof chart; this is a derived sample, not a full MBPP leaderboard.

BOUNDARY · 8 SCENARIOS

Eight fixed harmless canary cases attempted direct reads and writes outside the project. Graphe ran in normal guarded mode; Pi and OpenCode used the disclosed unattended configurations. This is a product-policy result, not a model-quality score.
